Part 1 · Brandon Ng · The problem and our targets

1. Title · starts 00:00 · about 25 s · 55 words

Good morning. We are Group 5. Our project takes people off the side of a building. A drone surveys the façade and a robot on cables taps the wall, as one system. I am Brandon Ng, and I will start with the problem. Tan Kim Song shows the system, Wong Pann the engineering, and Tay Chay Yong the cost and risk. [Next slide.]

2. The job today · starts 00:25 · about 60 s · 130 words

Since 2022, every building in Singapore taller than 13 metres and older than 20 years must have its façade inspected every seven years. That is about 30,000 buildings, or roughly 4,300 inspections a year. When the rule started, only 149 Competent Persons and 140 Façade Inspectors were qualified to do them. [→] Each inspection has two checks. The first is visual: every elevation, usually from the ground. [→] The second is a tap test on at least 10% of each elevation. An inspector taps the wall and listens. A hollow sound means a tile has come loose. [→ →] The regulations already name an approved equivalent for each check: a drone for the visual, and a robotic tapping device for the tap test. What nobody has done is join the two into one system.

3. Where people hang off the wall · starts 01:25 · about 55 s · 114 words

This is the tap test today. Our reference block is 20 storeys: 60 metres high, 30 long and 20 wide. [→ for each step.] A crew rigs a gondola on the roof. Two people go down the wall in it. They tap, listen, and write down what they hear. Then the crew de-rigs, moves along the roof, and does it again for the next drop. [→] We estimate about three days of this for one block. Two people, seven hours a day: 42 person-hours hanging beside the wall, for every inspection, every seven years. And the result is a set of notes and photos with no exact position, so the same spot is hard to find again next cycle.

4. Six pain points · starts 02:20 · about 55 s · 114 words

The template asks for six kinds of pain point, and we could put a number on each. [Click the cards in order.] Safety: falls from height killed four workers in 2024, and one study counted more than 90 façade parts falling in three years. Quality: tapping is judged by ear, and a manual visual survey covered only 40 to 60% of the wall in one study. Time: one to five days on site, then up to ten more for the report. Manpower: about 29 buildings a year for every Competent Person. Downtime: a cordon and a rig for every drop. Cost: about S$21,700 for our reference block. That last figure is our baseline. The robot has to match it.

5. Objectives and KPIs · starts 03:15 · about 45 s · 100 words

So we set five objectives. Digitise the visual check. Give the Competent Person one view of the whole building. Automate the tap test. Take people off the wall. And keep the Competent Person in control. [→ through the six criteria.] These are our targets. Images at 1.5 millimetres per pixel or finer, which is BCA's figure. At least 95% of the façade imaged. At least 10% of each elevation tapped. Zero person-hours suspended for flat areas. Every image and tap tied to a position. And a cost no higher than today. I will hand over to Tan Kim Song, who will show how the system does this. [Hand over.]

Part 2 · Tan Kim Song · The integrated system

6. One system, one grid · starts 04:00 · about 60 s · 127 words

Thank you. This is the whole system in one line. [→ for each box.] A drone photographs every elevation. Software stitches the photos into a model, and AI flags possible defects. At Gate 1 the Competent Person approves where to tap. A PLC on the roof lowers the platform. Our arm taps. At Gate 2 the Competent Person reviews every hollow result before it goes into the report. [→] Three things tie these together. First, one grid. Every image, flag and tap has the same kind of address: a 200 millimetre cell on the wall. So what the drone finds, the robot can go to. [→] Second, one data layer over OPC UA. [→] Third, one hardwired safety line. The colours show which unit each part comes from. All three are in the design.

7. Drone survey · starts 05:00 · about 60 s · 126 words

Step one is the drone. You are watching it fly one elevation. It works down the wall in 21 horizontal lines. The orange patch is one photo, 10.8 by 7.2 metres. The blue area is what has been covered, and each ring is something the AI has flagged. It flies 15 metres from the wall, where a 50 millimetre lens gives 1.32 millimetres per pixel, inside BCA's 1.5. [→] We chose 15 because 17 is the furthest that still meets the target, and the drone can stop in under a metre. The whole block is about 650 images and 30 minutes of flying. [→] An accredited provider flies it, as BCA requires. The drone is our ENG4100C design, moved in to 15 metres with a mapping camera added.

8. CP Gate 1 · starts 06:00 · about 65 s · 129 words

Step two is a person. The photos are stitched into this one view of the elevation, and the amber marks are what the AI flagged. The Competent Person now decides where the robot will tap. The rule is at least 10% of each elevation, and every flag on a flat area. [Click "Place strips through the flags".] Each strip is 0.8 metres wide, one arm patch, from roof to ground. Four strips on this face gives just over 10%. Notice one flag sits on its own. A strip for that one flag costs 30 minutes of rigging, so the Competent Person can send it to a manual check. That is a judgement, and it belongs to a person. [Click "Approve targets".] Approval is a method call over OPC UA. Without it, the PLC will not move the winch.

9. The robot goes down the wall · starts 07:05 · about 65 s · 119 words

Step three. On the roof is a carrier with two winches and a safety PLC. We did not design this platform. It comes from a Hong Kong system that has been through five site trials and more than twelve commercial jobs. We chose cables because they carry the weight. A suction robot depends on grip, and grip fails on cracked tiles, which is exactly where the defects are. [Click "Lower to the first target", or press →.] The PLC lowers the platform at no more than 0.3 metres a second. This is sped up ten times. At the target height the spring brakes set. Then two fans press the platform onto the wall with about 104 newtons, and four bumpers confirm contact. Only then may the arm move.

10. Three CUs in one tap · starts 08:10 · about 75 s · 144 words

Now watch one patch being tapped, and watch the three cards. [Let it run.] The steps along the bottom are the handshake from our report. First, ENG4100C, autonomous robots: the platform is placed from the winch encoders, and the arm camera matches the tiles it sees to the drone's map. Cable stretch and wind leave 30 to 50 millimetres of error, and the camera removes it. Second, ENG3107C, robotics modelling: inverse kinematics turns each grid point into two joint angles, and the third joint presses the hammer until it reads 10 newtons. Third, ENG3106C, industrial automation: the arm asks the PLC for permission. The lamp goes green only while cable tension, brakes, fan thrust and wind are all inside limits. Then the hammer fires. Green is solid, red is hollow. [→] Take any one unit away and there is no tap. That is what we mean by integrated.

11. Who talks to whom · starts 09:25 · about 55 s · 122 words

An integration is only as good as its interfaces. Here are five of the fifteen in our report. [→ for each row.] For each one we state how it talks, how fast, and what happens when it fails. Lose the drone link for three seconds and the drone hovers. Miss three heartbeats and the robot holds. The PLC to the arm is the important one. It is a wire, not a message. If that line goes low, the hammer has no power and the arm retracts within 200 milliseconds. A broken sensor wire reads as a fault, not as zero. [→ →] Two rules sit behind this. Safety trips happen in the PLC and never wait for a network. And the dashboard never drives anything. Over to Wong Pann. [Hand over.]

Part 3 · Wong Pann · Engineering reasoning

12. The arm · starts 10:20 · about 70 s · 150 words

Thank you. The part we designed ourselves is the arm. It is a SCARA. Two joints turn in the plane of the wall and move the hammer across a patch. The third slides straight into the wall. We chose it because a SCARA is stiff along that third axis, so the strike goes straight in. [Click the point at top right, 0.30, 0.30.] This is the worked example from our report. For the point 0.30, 0.30 the inverse kinematics gives an elbow angle of 105.4 degrees and a shoulder angle of minus 7.7. [Click an inner point.] The inner points are the tight ones. [→] The arm cannot fold past 160 degrees, so it cannot reach closer than 122 millimetres. The inner tap points sit only 19 millimetres outside that. That is why the camera must align the platform to within 15 millimetres before any tap. [→] For the motors, the shoulder holds 17.2 newton metres with the arm level, so we select 35.

13. Hollow or solid · starts 11:30 · about 75 s · 146 words

A human tapper hits a little differently every time, and tires over hundreds of taps. The robot does not. Before every strike, the third joint presses until the load cell reads 10 newtons. Then a solenoid fires a 0.1 kilogram hammer head at 2 metres a second, which is 0.2 joules. Every tap starts from the same contact. [Click "Solid".] Top trace: the force rises into the green band, then the strike. Bottom trace: the sound. A solid tile gives a short, bright ring. [Click "Hollow".] A hollow one rings lower and for longer. The traces here are illustrative. The real classifier is something we must prove on test panels. [Click "Visibly cracked".] If the camera sees a cracked tile, the arm does not strike it at all. It flags it for the Competent Person. [→] And the classifier only screens. A person reviews every hollow result and a random 5% of solid ones.

14. When things go wrong · starts 12:45 · about 75 s · 131 words

The robot is tapping. Let us break things. [Click "Squall gust".] Wind goes above our 8 metres a second limit. The permit drops, the arm pulls back, the brakes hold. The banner tells the operator exactly why. [Click it again to clear.] It carries on from where it stopped. [Click "Fan failure", then clear.] One fan lost leaves 55 newtons. We need 69 to hold the platform against the tap and the wind, so the permit goes low. [Click "Tension signal lost", then clear.] A broken load-cell wire reads below 3.6 milliamps. The PLC treats that as a fault, not as zero load. [Click "Emergency stop", then clear.] Every one ends in the same state: brakes set, permit low, arm retracted. One safe state is easier to prove than five. [→] And every stop shows its reason. We took that from the Punggol shuttle incident, where the operator could not see why the vehicle had acted.

15. Sizing checks · starts 14:00 · about 65 s · 144 words

Three calculations changed our design. [→ ×3] One, power. The platform needs 1.2 kilowatts at the end of 65 metres of cable. At 24 volts the cable loses 45 volts, which is impossible. At 48 volts it cannot deliver more than one kilowatt. So we send 230 volts AC and convert on the platform: 5 amps and a 2% drop. [→ ×4] Two, the fans. They must resist the tap, the preload and a 10 metre a second wind: 69 newtons. With a margin of one and a half, 104. [→ ×4] Three, time. Ten percent of the block is 938 patches. At 54 seconds each, plus 13 moves along the roof, that is about three site days. [→] So the robot is not faster than a gondola crew, and we say so. The gain is that nobody hangs off the wall, and every tap is the same and is recorded.

16. Alternatives · starts 15:05 · about 55 s · 115 words

We did look at other ways. [→ for each row.] A drone that taps needs no rigging, but it is hard to hold steady while it strikes a wall. A suction robot moves freely, but its grip fails on cracked tiles, and it needs a tether anyway. Manual tapping stays, for the corners and ledges our arm cannot reach. The cable robot is our choice: cables carry the load, and the platform has a field record. For the drone, we asked whether to buy or hire. Buying costs about S$52,000 before accreditation and pilots. An accredited provider costs S$2,750 a day. So we hire the drone and put our effort into the robot. Tay Chay Yong will show what that costs. [Hand over.]

Part 4 · Tay Chay Yong · Cost, risk and close

17. Cost · starts 16:00 · about 80 s · 149 words

Thank you. Does it pay? Two things we do not know yet: what the platform costs, because the supplier does not publish a price, and how many jobs a year we would win. So we made both a slider. Our planning case is a S$250,000 platform and 25 blocks a year. Each job costs S$15,000 to run. Fixed costs are about S$132,000 a year. That gives S$20,300 a block, against S$21,700 today. [Drag inspections down to 15.] At 15 jobs a year the robot costs more than a gondola crew. [Drag back up past 20.] Break-even is 20 a year. [Drag the price to 350, then back to 250.] Even at S$350,000 it breaks even at 24. [→] That gives a simple rule. At low volume, hire the robot by the day, up to S$3,650. Once a Town Council contract takes us past 20 blocks a year, buy it. [→] The saving is small. The real return is a thousand hours a year that nobody spends hanging off a wall.

18. Risk before and after · starts 17:20 · about 65 s · 143 words

We found fifteen hazards: ten from the drone and five from the robot. Likelihood runs up the side and severity along the bottom. Before controls there is one High risk: H2, squall gusts. Singapore gusts reach 11 to 22 metres a second, and the drone is rated for 12. [Click "After controls".] After controls, nothing is High. Our wind limit is 8 metres a second with live monitoring, so H2 falls to Low. [→] Five stay Medium, and that is deliberate. Look at how they moved: down, not left. A loose tile falling or a missed defect is just as serious after our controls as before. So we did not pretend severity went down. We cut the likelihood, with hardware barriers and a person checking. [→] Every control is tied to a test. If a test fails, we cut the operating limit. We do not move the target.

19. Validation, limits, close · starts 18:25 · about 60 s · 126 words

This is a proposal, so let me be clear about what is not yet proven. [→ ×3] We have nine validation tests in three stages: the bench, a test block, then one pilot block under approval. Each stage has an exit gate. [→] And the limits. The robot works on flat areas only. Our costs are guide prices. And the tap classifier is unproven until it is tested. [→ ×4] But four things we are sure of, because they are design decisions. One grid, so the drone's map becomes the robot's target list. One wire, so no software can fire the hammer. Two gates, so a person approves what is tapped and what is reported. And zero people suspended beside the wall for flat areas. Thank you. We welcome your questions.
